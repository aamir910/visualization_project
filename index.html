02 · Before we begin

First, thank you.

This study exists because you sat with me and spoke openly about a course that was still very fresh. The quotes you'll see today are yours, anonymized. They are here to honour what you said, not to single anyone out.

03 · Section 01 — Problem

The question we asked

What happens in the seams between disciplines — between teachers, between intention and delivery?

Premise

Interdisciplinary work is widely seen as a driver of innovation, but it is also widely reported as difficult to coordinate.

Reframe

Treat challenges as systemic contradictions in course design, not individual failings.

04 · The lens

CHAT-3G

Cultural-Historical Activity Theory, third generation. Engeström's framework treats any human activity as seven interconnected components. The useful part isn't the components themselves — it's that the framework forces you to look at the spaces between them. That's where friction lives.

Method: 14 faculty interviews, transcribed and coded in NVivo against the seven components.

05–08 · Section 02 — Diagnosis

Findings

Who, and toward whatRoles evolved reactively. The goal kept moving.

◆ Subject — the faculty

Identity in motion: mentors, late entrants, and "protectors of the framework."

People joined late. Roles shifted mid-course. Some described themselves as guarding a structure they hadn't built.

"I think my role has evolved into something that I gotta try and protect the framework we have established." — Faculty participant
◆ Object — the goal

Faculty wanted innovation. Some students wanted their discipline.

The course intended creative, interdisciplinary projects. That intention met students who, in places, expected discipline-specific training.

"Some of these students felt like they were being forced to do something else that wasn't really what they signed up for." — Faculty participant

The sharpest frictionWithout rules, the labor distribution couldn't hold.

◆ Rules — formal & informal

"Maybe a bit too little structure."

Roles, schedules, and communication norms had to be improvised. Different lead teachers gave different information; lines of authority were unclear.

"Everything from roles to communication guidelines to responsibilities — everything had to be built." — Faculty participant
◆ Division of Labor

Double roles for some. Underutilization for others.

Course teacher and village leader at once for some; uncertain scope and unused expertise for others. Many learned their assignments only after students had begun.

"Those [planning and scheduling] were a bit improvised. I didn't know much about how it was going to be conducted." — Faculty participant

The connective tissueLocal belonging. Global fragmentation.

◆ Tools

Channels existed. A shared way to use them did not.

Teams, email, and frameworks like Prisma were all in play, but used informally. Information scattered; cognitive load grew.

"Messages and documents were scattered across different channels." — Faculty participant
◆ Community

Real collaboration happened in the hallway.

Villages developed strong internal identities — generative inside, walls outside. The most useful coordination often happened around the lunch table, not in scheduled meetings.

"Teacher collaboration happened informally rather than as part of a planned system." — Faculty participant

Where systems meetA theory–practice gap, named by faculty themselves.

◆ Administrative ↔ Pedagogical

Decisions made above the classroom collided with delivery inside it.

Budget cuts removed planned guest lectures mid-course. Pedagogical frameworks were promoted institutionally without the structural support to implement them.

"There was a notable theory-practice gap — pedagogical ideals without sufficient structural or organizational support." — From the analysis
◆ Disciplinary ↔ Disciplinary

Different methodologies, different feedback cultures, no shared vocabulary yet.

Even adjacent disciplines reported needing time to learn each other's language and definitions before real collaboration could begin.

"Even if we're closely related discipline-wise, we're all apart when it comes to methodology and feedback." — Faculty participant

09 · Synthesis — the collaboration paradox

The same friction that frustrated you also produced what you were proud of.

Unproductive friction
  • Role ambiguity
  • Communication overload
  • Last-minute improvisation
  • Unequal workloads
  • A weakened sense of unified community
Productive friction
  • Self-organization
  • Negotiated roles
  • Cross-disciplinary translation
  • Student projects nobody had specified
  • Genuinely impressive work — by your own accounts

10 · Section 03 — Recommendations

Remove the friction that wastes you. Keep the friction that teaches.

01

Define roles before launch.

In writing, with hours and scope. Role ambiguity was the most direct cause of frustration and burnout.

02

One channel for course-wide communication.

Pick it, protect it, and stop the scatter across Teams, email, and side-channels.

03

A formal community of practice for faculty.

Give the lunch-table conversations a scheduled home. Don't leave coordination to chance.

04

Align the institutional layer with the teaching layer.

Budgets, pedagogical frameworks, and scheduling — settled before the course begins, not during.
